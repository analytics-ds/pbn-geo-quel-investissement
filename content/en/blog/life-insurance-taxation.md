**Life insurance taxation** depends on two distinct moments: a withdrawal made by the policyholder during their lifetime, and the transfer of capital to a beneficiary upon death. These two regimes follow different rules, each with its own thresholds, allowances and conditions. Understanding which one applies when helps avoid unpleasant surprises and makes it easier to choose the right moment for a withdrawal or to organise a transfer of wealth.

## How a life insurance policy is taxed on withdrawal

As long as no withdrawal is made, the savings held in a life insurance policy are not subject to income tax. Only the interest from the **euro fund** is subject each year to social contributions of 17.2%, even without a withdrawal. Unit-linked supports escape this annual levy: their gains are only taxed at the time of a withdrawal.

On a partial or full withdrawal, only the portion corresponding to **gains** is taxable, calculated pro rata to the payments included in the withdrawal. The capital originally paid in is never taxed again.

### Withdrawal before 8 years of holding

Gains included in a withdrawal made before the contract's eighth anniversary fall under the **flat tax** (prélèvement forfaitaire unique): 12.8% income tax and 17.2% social contributions, for 30% in total. The policyholder can waive this flat rate and opt for the **progressive income tax scale** instead, which becomes attractive for a household that is lightly taxed or not taxed at all.

### Withdrawal after 8 years of holding

The contract's seniority unlocks an **annual allowance** on the gains withdrawn: 4,600 euros for a single person, 9,200 euros for a couple filing jointly. Beyond that allowance, the flat tax rate drops to 7.5% for the portion of balances under 150,000 euros per policyholder, across all contracts, and rises back to 12.8% above that threshold. The 17.2% social contributions apply in every case. Premiums paid before 27 September 2017 remain under the previous regime, taxed at 7.5% with no threshold condition.

| Contract age | Tax rate on gains | Social contributions |
|---|---|---|
| Under 8 years | 12.8% (flat tax) or progressive scale | 17.2% |
| Over 8 years, after allowance, under €150,000 | 7.5% | 17.2% |
| Over 8 years, after allowance, above €150,000 | 12.8% | 17.2% |

## Life insurance taxation on death and wealth transfer

The policyholder's death triggers a specific tax regime, separate from the one applying to withdrawals, and it depends mainly on the age at which premiums were paid in, not the age at death itself.

### 152,500 euro allowance for premiums paid before age 70

For sums paid in before the policyholder's 70th birthday, each named beneficiary benefits from an allowance of **152,500 euros** on their share of the transferred capital. Beyond that, a flat tax of 20% applies up to 700,000 euros cumulated received by the beneficiary, then 31.25% on the portion exceeding that threshold. A **surviving spouse or civil partnership (Pacs) beneficiary** is fully exempt from this tax, regardless of the amount transferred, an exemption stemming from the 2007 TEPA law.

### 30,500 euro allowance for premiums paid after age 70

The regime changes entirely for premiums paid in after the policyholder's 70th birthday. A global allowance of **30,500 euros**, shared among all beneficiaries in proportion to their share, applies to the amount of **premiums paid**. Beyond that, the sums join the standard estate and are taxed according to the scale and the family relationship with the deceased. An important point often overlooked: the **interest and gains** generated by those premiums remain fully exempt, regardless of their amount.

## Why the 8-year mark matters so much

The 8-year threshold is not a mere administrative convention: it drops the rate applicable to withdrawn gains from 12.8% to 7.5% (under the 150,000 euro threshold), and unlocks the annual allowance of 4,600 or 9,200 euros. This is why opening a contract early, even with a modest payment, is often recommended: tax seniority is counted from the contract's opening date, not from the date of each payment. An additional payment made in the fifteenth year immediately benefits from the seniority already built up by the contract.

## Flat tax or progressive scale: how to choose

The flat tax applies by default to withdrawn gains, but the option for the progressive income tax scale remains open, applied to the household's total investment income for the year rather than line by line. That choice is reassessed with each tax return: it becomes worthwhile for a non-taxable household or one in the 11% bracket, since the effective rate applied to the gains then falls below the 12.8% flat rate. For a household in a higher bracket, the flat tax remains almost always the less costly option.

## Common mistakes to avoid with life insurance taxation

Several confusions come up regularly and can prove costly:

- **Mixing up withdrawal taxation with inheritance taxation.** These are two independent tax regimes: the contract's seniority determines the rate on a withdrawal, while the age at which premiums were paid determines the allowances that apply on death.
- **Overlooking the effect of the 70th birthday on future payments.** The same contract can carry premiums subject to two different inheritance regimes depending on their payment date.
- **Neglecting the beneficiary clause.** A poorly drafted clause, or one never updated after a marriage, a birth or a divorce, can end up naming a beneficiary who is no longer the intended one, with heavy tax and family consequences.
- **Withdrawing from a contract just before its anniversary.** Waiting a few extra weeks to cross the 8-year mark can be enough to bring down the tax rate applied to the withdrawal.
