Looking for the **best platform to invest €5,000 in tech** means answering a prior question first: gaining exposure to tech giants that are already listed, or to companies that are not yet on the stock market. The former can be bought from any broker through an ETF. The latter, startups and scale-ups backed by venture capital, stay closed to individuals without a specialised intermediary.

This comparison focuses on the second route, **unlisted tech**, and on the platforms that make it accessible with €5,000. Four players are reviewed: Fundora, Anaxago, Ramify and Moonfare.

## Why unlisted tech is hard to access with €5,000

A large share of value creation in tech now happens before the IPO. Venture-backed companies stay private longer than twenty years ago and raise amounts that used to be available only on public markets.

### An institutional ticket out of reach for individuals

The most sought-after venture capital and growth funds target institutional investors. A direct subscription usually requires between **€200,000 and €1 million**, that is 40 to 200 times a €5,000 budget.

This threshold is not arbitrary. A fund manages a limited number of subscribers and requires large commitments to keep administrative costs down. Individuals are therefore excluded by design, however well they know the sector.

### What tech ETFs do not cover

A listed **technology ETF**, for example a PEA-eligible Nasdaq-100 ETF charging 0.30% a year, gives exposure to large companies already on the stock market. It holds no unlisted startup. Both approaches complement each other, as explained in the comparison between [private equity and the stock market](/en/blog/private-equity-vs-stock-market/).

## Fundora, the platform best suited to a €5,000 budget

Fundora is a French platform giving individual investors access to private equity funds usually reserved for institutions. Its selection focuses on funds ranked in the global top quartile. The platform reports more than 40,000 users and more than €100 million raised. Strategies are presented on the [Fundora website](https://www.fundora.fr/).

The mechanism relies on two structures. The platform takes an allocation in a target fund through an **FPCI**, a French professional private equity fund, then pools subscriptions from several individuals into a dedicated **SPV**. This shared vehicle reaches the minimum required by the target fund, which no single subscriber could do alone.

The platform does not manage the money itself. Fundora identifies and offers the strategies, with actual management carried out by Kyoseil Asset Management under the mandate. This portfolio management company is authorised by the French financial markets authority under number GP-99040.

### Tech strategies offered by Fundora

Several of the platform's strategies focus directly on technology. Their target multiples are management objectives, not guaranteed returns:

- **YC Venture**: startups from the Y Combinator accelerator, target of 5 to 7 times the capital invested.
- **Nextwave AI** and **Genesis AI Venture**: artificial intelligence, target of 6 to 8 times.
- **Cyber Intelligence Venture**: cybersecurity, target of 5.5 times.
- **Alcove Semi-conducteurs**: semiconductors, target of 3.8 times.
- **Atlas Space Ventures**: space, target of 4.5 times.
- **DeepScale Growth**: global tech scale-ups, target of 3.5 times.

### Key features

- **Entry threshold**: sharply lowered compared with a direct subscription, the minimum amount varying with the strategy open for subscription.
- **Direct access**: no intermediate insurance wrapper, so no extra layer of contract fees.
- **Range depth**: more than 35 strategies launched, across venture, growth, secondaries, buyouts and private debt.
- **Diversification**: each target fund holds several dozen companies, which a single crowdfunding ticket cannot offer.

## Anaxago, Ramify, Moonfare: what the other platforms offer

The other three players give access to unlisted assets through very different mechanisms. None is bad in itself, but each one fits a specific need.

### Anaxago: one startup per ticket

Anaxago works through **equity crowdfunding**: the investor becomes a minority shareholder in a startup selected by an investment committee, with a ticket of around €1,000 per deal. With €5,000, four or five companies can be backed at most.

This concentration is the main point to watch. In venture capital, a small minority of holdings generates most of a portfolio's gains, and an investor with only five holdings has little chance of owning the one that drives performance.

### Ramify: unlisted assets inside a wrapper

Ramify includes unlisted funds in life insurance, retirement plans or securities accounts. The ticket starts at €1,000 on some tax funds and rises to €10,000 on performance funds, which limits the choice for a €5,000 budget.

The wrapper brings a known tax framework. In return, the investor holds a product listed by the insurer, and the contract charges its own fees on top of the fund's. The rules of this route are covered in the guide to [FCPR, the French private equity fund](/en/blog/fcpr-french-private-equity-fund/).

### Moonfare: institutional funds from €50,000

Moonfare opens funds from major international managers from **€50,000** on most funds. The institutional access is real, but the ticket is ten times the budget studied here. The platform targets investors with established financial wealth.

## Returns and risks of unlisted tech

Private equity has historically outperformed French listed markets over the long term. The annual benchmark study confirms this for France.

> "Over the last ten years, the IRR of private equity stands at 10.7% net per year, against 9.5% for the CAC 40, 8.9% for the CAC All Tradable and 5.5% for the CAC Mid & Small."
> France Invest and EY, study on the net performance of French private equity at the end of 2025, June 2026

This average hides a very wide dispersion. In French growth capital, top-quartile funds exceed a 20% net IRR while bottom-quartile funds end up in negative territory. Fund selection therefore matters more than the choice of asset class.

The risk of capital loss is real. A startup can fail and return nothing. A venture fund offsets these failures with a few major successes, provided it holds enough companies.

Liquidity is close to zero. A venture strategy ties up capital for **8 to 12 years**, a growth or secondary strategy somewhat less. The money invested must be money that will not be needed over that period.

## How to split €5,000 with an unlisted tech allocation

Unlisted assets replace neither the emergency fund nor liquid investments. They come on top, as a minority share of the amount. The principles of this allocation are set out in the guide to [diversifying asset classes](/en/blog/diversifying-asset-classes/).

| Profile | Unlisted tech | Listed tech (ETF) | Other liquid assets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cautious | €0 to €500 (0 to 10%) | €1,000 | €3,500 to €4,000 |
| Balanced | €500 to €1,000 (10 to 20%) | €1,500 | €2,500 to €3,000 |
| Dynamic | €1,000 to €1,500 (20 to 30%) | €2,000 | €1,500 to €2,000 |

This table assumes that the emergency fund, usually three to six months of expenses, is already held in a savings account. Amounts are indicative and should match each investor's horizon and risk tolerance.

For a first step into the asset class, the stages are explained in the article on [how to invest in private equity](/en/blog/private-equity-how-to-invest/). The full overview of [private equity platforms for retail investors](/en/blog/private-equity-platforms-retail-investors/) widens the comparison beyond tech.

### Mistakes to avoid

- **Betting everything on one startup**: with €5,000, a single ticket concentrates all the risk on one company.
- **Investing the emergency fund**: money placed in unlisted assets cannot be recovered for several years.
- **Confusing target and return**: a 5x target multiple is a management intention, not a promise.
- **Ignoring the vintage**: spreading subscriptions over several years smooths out the effect of a poor market cycle.
